The learning planner should be able to answer a fundamental question:

"What is the minimum amount of learning this person needs to reach their goal, given what they already know?"

And I agree with your constraint: we should not make users manually select the courses or concepts they already understand. That's tedious, unreliable, and becomes completely impractical when a learning roadmap contains hundreds of concepts.

I recommend building an Adaptive Diagnostic Engine inside the learning planner.

Rather than testing every prerequisite individually, the system should intelligently select a small number of questions that reveal the most about the learner's existing knowledge.

The idea is to use three complementary mechanisms:

Conversational profiling: Understand the learner's background through a short, natural interaction.

Adaptive knowledge assessment: Ask strategically selected questions and use the answers to estimate which competencies the learner possesses.

Continuous verification: Update those estimates as the learner progresses, correcting any incorrect assumptions made during onboarding.

The third part matters particularly. We shouldn't need to perfectly understand the learner during their first five minutes on the platform. The system should become more accurate as it observes actual learning performance.

Let's design how this would work.

How difficult is it?

How much information could it provide?

How long is it expected to take?

How reliable is its scoring?

Ideally, the planner asks the question that reduces the most relevant uncertainty per unit of time.

We don't need to implement sophisticated Bayesian inference immediately.

For our initial project, I'd use a deterministic question-selection algorithm with a simple uncertainty model, leveraging the graph structure.

For example, prioritize questions that:

This is a major design decision.

These percentages are illustrative, not statistically calibrated probabilities.

The evidence status is as important as the score.

For planning purposes, classify each concept into three states:

Verified mastery

The learner has provided sufficient direct evidence against the concept's learning objectives. The planner can skip the associated instructional material.

Provisional mastery

Evidence suggests the learner probably knows the concept, but confidence is limited. The planner may provisionally bypass it while retaining a lightweight verification checkpoint.

Learning required

Evidence indicates a knowledge gap, or the available evidence is insufficient to safely skip a critical prerequisite.

This prevents one lucky correct answer from causing the system to skip an entire foundational course.

Also, the graph itself should remain intact. The learner receives a personalized view with node states, rather than permanently deleting skipped concepts from the DAG.

4. What about users who already know almost everything?
This is where the approach becomes powerful.

Suppose someone has a bachelor's degree in physics and wants to learn introductory Quantum Computing.

The user provides this background conversationally.

The system can use that information as an initial hypothesis, without treating the degree itself as proof of mastery.

It begins with advanced diagnostic questions around likely entry points.

If the learner demonstrates strong knowledge, it does not waste time asking elementary questions about vectors and differentiation.

Instead, it probes the uncertain boundary between their existing competencies and their target competencies.

In contrast, if someone with little physics background struggles with those same questions, the diagnostic moves toward foundational material.

The diagnostic should move both upward and downward through the prerequisite graph based on evidence.

There is one nuance: success on an advanced question doesn't necessarily establish mastery of all its prerequisites, especially when the learner may have learned a shortcut or a narrowly defined procedure. We need occasional checks of high-impact prerequisites and separate checks for independent branches.

5. The diagnostic doesn't have to finish before learning begins
This is probably my favorite part of the design.

Why force users through an exhaustive entry examination?

We can use progressive diagnosis.

Initially, the learner completes a short adaptive assessment. For the MVP, perhaps target approximately 5–10 minutes, with a skip option. That duration is a product-design target, not a guarantee of diagnostic accuracy.

Then the planner generates the initial roadmap.

As the learner works through concepts, the teaching and assessment systems continuously produce additional mastery evidence.

The planner updates the roadmap accordingly.

For example:

During onboarding, the platform provisionally considers the learner proficient in calculus.

Two weeks later, the learner struggles with a differential-equation step while studying wave functions.

The assessment engine identifies a possible prerequisite gap.

The planner inserts a targeted differential-equations review into the roadmap.

Once the gap is resolved, the original learning journey resumes.

This approach also handles learners who have forgotten material they previously understood.

The planner is continuously estimating readiness, not assigning a permanent educational label during onboarding.

The diagnostic agent can coordinate conversational interactions, but the underlying graph state and mastery updates should be handled by these controlled components.

For course-level planning, we can retain the course DAG that you originally envisioned. Internally, each course would map to several assessable competencies.

For example, the planner should only mark Linear Algebra Foundations as fully skippable when its required learning objectives are sufficiently covered. Otherwise, it could skip the familiar lessons and recommend only the missing parts.

This gives us course-level simplicity in the UI with concept-level diagnostic precision.

What I would implement in version one
A curated prerequisite graph.

A tagged diagnostic question bank with answer keys and rubrics.

Adaptive question selection based on graph position, uncertainty and expected diagnostic value.

Learner-state records containing mastery estimates and evidence.

Course skipping with provisional and verified states.

Automatic roadmap revision when later assessments reveal missing prerequisites.

I would not start with full Item response theory calibration or neural knowledge tracing. Statistical CAT models typically require calibrated question banks, and their properties should not be assumed reliable without suitable response data.

My recommended design
The learning planner should combine hierarchical adaptive assessment, prerequisite-aware mastery inference, and continuous diagnostic feedback.

This provides a meaningful solution to your concern without making users manually configure their knowledge profiles or forcing them to take an examination covering every concept.

And it gives our project a substantial algorithmic component beyond calling an LLM.

The key next design problem is how the Mastery Estimator propagates evidence through the prerequisite graph. If a learner demonstrates understanding of concept C, how strongly should that influence our beliefs about prerequisite concepts A and B? And how do we prevent those inferences from being overconfident?

That is where I think we can make the planner both intelligent and reliable.

Understood. We'll treat this strictly as a Software Engineering course project, not a research project. The objective is to build a functional, reasonably scalable system without introducing unnecessary complexity.

Reviewing only your six decisions, all six are feasible, but three have functional or architectural issues that should be addressed before implementation.

Review of the six decisions
1. Selecting pedagogical models based on learner context
No major architectural problem
This is a valid design.

The Tutor receives learner context, selects an appropriate teaching method, and generates its response accordingly.

One potential issue: What happens when the selected pedagogical method is ineffective?

For example, the Tutor selects Socratic questioning, but the learner repeatedly fails to understand.

Minimal fix: Allow the Tutor to switch teaching strategies based on the learner's responses. You don't need another agent or a sophisticated strategy-selection algorithm.

The Tutor itself can handle this.

Verdict: Keep as proposed.

2. Update learner context after every X tokens and every DAG node
Functional issue
The main issue is that these two triggers serve different purposes.

Every X tokens: Useful for compressing conversation history and retaining important information.

After every DAG node: Useful for updating knowledge mastery, progress, and misconceptions.

The problem: Suppose a learner reveals an important misconception halfway through a node, but the token threshold has not been reached.

The Tutor might continue using outdated learner context.

Minimal fix: Keep your two triggers, but also update learner context after important assessment results or significant learning events.

You can implement this through your existing Learning Context AI.

There is no need for another component.

Verdict: Modify the update triggers slightly.

3. Assessment after every DAG node
No major architectural problem
This is a reasonable design.

The learner completes a node, takes an assessment, and proceeds to the next node based on the result.

One missing functional decision: What happens when the learner fails?

The architecture needs a defined progression rule.

For example:

Node completed → Assessment
Pass

Mark node complete and unlock eligible successors

Fail

Send feedback to Tutor for remediation and reassessment

For the first version, you don't need to regenerate the DAG whenever an assessment fails.

Simply let the Tutor explain the weak areas and then reassess.

Verdict: Keep, but define failure handling.

4. Recursive questioning before generating the curriculum
Potential functional problem
The problem is the stopping condition.

If you instruct the Planner to continue asking questions until it has sufficient knowledge, it might:

Ask too many questions.

Ask redundant questions.

Never confidently determine whether it has enough information.

Minimal fix: Give the Planner a maximum number of diagnostic questions, such as 5–10, and allow it to stop earlier if it believes sufficient information has been collected.

Also let the learner skip the diagnostic phase.

The generated curriculum can be adjusted later if the initial level estimate was incorrect.

Verdict: Keep recursive questioning, but bound it.

5. Cache educational resources across learners
Architectural issue
This is a good idea, but your current description mixes two different things:

Caching educational resources discovered through internet searches.

Reusing the context generated during another learner's planning process.

These are not equivalent.

For example, when Learner A studies Linear Algebra, their curriculum may be personalized for someone who already knows calculus.

Learner B may have a completely different background.

Reusing Learner A's curriculum directly would be incorrect.

Minimal fix: Cache subject-level resources, not learner-specific planning context.

For example:

Topic: Linear Algebra

Cached information:
- Resource URLs
- Resource descriptions
- Relevant concept/topic tags
- Source metadata
- Cached content where permitted
When another learner requests Linear Algebra, the Planner searches the existing cache before using web search.

The Planner still generates a personalized DAG using Learner B's context.

Another issue is caching incorrect information. For the MVP, use a small set of trusted educational sources and store source references alongside cached content.

You do not need a separate knowledge-verification agent.

Verdict: Keep, but clearly separate shared resource data from personal learner context.

6. Learning Planner generates DAG and curriculum within each node
No major architectural problem
This division of responsibility makes sense.

The Planner determines:

What concepts to study.

Their prerequisite dependencies.

The order of learning.

The curriculum or learning objectives associated with each node.

The Tutor is responsible for actually teaching those objectives.

One potential issue: Generating an entire highly detailed curriculum for a large DAG in a single call could produce inconsistent results and high token costs.

Minimal fix: Generate the DAG first, along with brief learning objectives for each node.

When the learner activates a node, generate its detailed teaching content using the existing objectives and learner context.

This still respects your original decision that the Planner defines the curriculum.

Also validate that the resulting graph has no cycles and references valid prerequisite nodes.

Verdict: Keep, with lightweight initial node specifications.

Two additional architectural issues in your diagram
Apart from those six decisions, I noticed two concerns in the actual component interactions.

A. Who is responsible for updating learner context?

Your diagram suggests both the Tutor and Assessment Engine provide information to Learning Context AI, which subsequently updates User Context.

That is fine, but establish one simple rule:

Only Learning Context AI updates the stored learner context.

The Tutor and Assessment Engine should provide observations rather than directly modifying the database. This avoids conflicting updates.

B. How does the Planner know when replanning is necessary?

Your diagram shows the Planner generating the DAG initially, but its behavior after curriculum generation isn't clearly defined.

Suppose the learner struggles repeatedly with a prerequisite concept.

For the MVP, you don't need automatic DAG restructuring. You can initially retain the graph and let the Tutor provide remediation.

However, the system should support an explicit replan request when the learner changes their goal or the initial curriculum is clearly unsuitable.

This can be handled by the existing Planner.

Final recommendation
I would approve all six design decisions with the small modifications above.

There is no need right now for additional specialized agents, advanced knowledge tracing, reinforcement learning, complex graph databases, or an elaborate knowledge-verification pipeline.

The one area I'd pay particular attention to is Decision 2: Learner Context.

That context is shared across the Planner, Tutor, and Assessment Engine. If it is inaccurate, outdated, or poorly structured, all three components can make incorrect decisions.

For a feasible course project, a structured learner profile combined with a compact conversation summary should be sufficient.

Your current architecture is workable. The next useful design task is specifying the exact data exchanged between the Planner, Tutor, Assessment Engine, and Learning Context AI, because that will reveal any remaining integration problems before coding.
